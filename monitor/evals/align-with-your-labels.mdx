<Note>
**Before you start:** a dataset with your grades in a column (see [Build datasets](/monitor/evals/datasets-prompts)), an evaluator that reads it, and the **Operator** role or above.
</Note>

An evaluator you have not checked against your own judgment measures something, but you do not know what. The check
is simple: grade a few dozen records yourself, run the evaluator on the same records, and count how often it agrees.
Do it before the evaluator judges production, and again whenever you change the prompt or the model.

## Your labels are the ground truth

Your labels live in a dataset, as a column of your choosing, one grade per row in the evaluator's own words:
`hallucinated` or `factual`, `pass` or `fail`. Three ways to get there:

- **Capture from traces**, then grade. Capture a uniform sample of real records into the dataset, read each, and fill
  in the column. Fifty rows is a sound start; make sure both labels are well represented, and include the near misses,
  the answers that look right and are not.
- **Import** a file your reviewers already graded, with the grade as a column. A column named `expected` is the
  expected answer; your grade can be any other column, such as `expected_label`.
- **Write rows** by hand for the cases that matter most and come up least: the edge of the policy, the question the
  agent must refuse.

One rule for the labels: use the evaluator's label names exactly. A row labelled with a word the grader cannot answer
is reported, not compared.

## Measure agreement

In the evaluator's editor, under **Configuration**, pick the dataset as the data source and set **Compare with your
labels** to your column. The status line says how many rows carry a label. Then select **Test on 20 rows**.

The result reads **Agrees with you on 18 of 20 rows (90%)**, green at 90% or more, amber at 70%, red below. Under it:

- **The grid**, your label down the side and the grader's across the top. The diagonal is agreement; everything else
  is a kind of mistake you can name, such as "it calls grounded answers hallucinated".
- **The disagreements**, each row with your label, the grader's label and its reasoning. This is where the fix is: the
  reasoning tells you whether the judge misread the record, applied a rule you did not mean, or found something you
  missed.
- **More stats**: for each label, **When the grader says it, it is right** (precision) and **Of yours, it finds**
  (recall), and **Agreement beyond chance (Cohen's κ)**. A grader that always answers the commonest label agrees often
  by luck; κ counts only what it gets right beyond that. It reads `worse than chance`, `slight`, `fair`, `moderate`,
  `strong` or `near perfect`, and is not measurable when one label was given throughout.

Rows the grader could not judge, or that have no label of yours, are left out of the count and said so. A test keeps
nothing; it costs one model call a row for a model judge, and nothing for a code evaluator.

An experiment with **Saved answers** as its source and **Your labels** set measures the same agreement over the whole
dataset, and keeps it on the experiment's scorecard. A multi-question judge cannot be compared this way: your label is
one answer and it gives several. Check each question as a plain LLM-as-a-judge first.

## Fix the judge

Work from the disagreements, one cause at a time, and test again after each change.

| What you see | Likely cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| It marks good answers bad for facts it "knows" | No source of truth, so the model grades on its own knowledge | Give it the context or an expected answer, and say the reference is the whole truth |
| Two labels both fit many rows | The label definitions overlap | Define each label by what the other is not, in the prompt |
| It is right for the wrong reason | The question is too broad | Ask one narrower question; move the rest to a multi-question judge |
| It answers a label you did not offer, or none | A model that cannot call tools, with **Strict answers** on | Turn **Strict answers** off, or change the model |
| It flips on the same row between tests | Temperature above 0 | Set **Temperature** to 0 |
| Your reviewers disagree with each other | The rule is unclear to people too | Write the rule down and regrade; a judge cannot agree with a label that is not consistent |
| High agreement, low κ | One label dominates the set | Add rows of the rarer label before trusting the number |
| It is good on short answers, wrong on long ones | The record is cut or the question is buried | Put the question at the top and the record under headings; for a run, judge the step rather than the whole trace |

When to stop: agreement you would accept from a new reviewer, with a κ of **strong** or better and no systematic
mistake left in the grid. Then save, and run the evaluator on traffic.

## Keep it aligned

Agreement decays as the agent, its prompts and its traffic change. Three habits keep it honest:

- **Keep the dataset growing.** When a monitor or a reader finds a new failure, add the record to the dataset with your
  label, so the next test covers it. **Capture from traces** adds nothing already there.
- **Retest after any change** to the template, labels, model or temperature. **Update from template** is a change.
- **Spot-check production.** Read a handful of **Scores** a week, with the reasoning. Where you disagree, that record
  is a new dataset row.
